# Finish One Piece of Office Work Before Thinking About Rollout

## Start with an Ordinary Example

<span id="claim-workflow-core-01" data-claim-id="claim-workflow-core-01"></span>If you are using Qwen Work for the first time, treat it as a work assistant that helps you organize materials, build a first draft, and find gaps. It can get you to a revisable version faster, but it cannot confirm facts for you, decide reporting conventions, or send content on your behalf. The starting point for ordinary office users is simple: say you need to put together a weekly report—collect chat records, meeting notes, and scattered progress into a first draft with clear priorities that you can keep editing; for meeting wrap-up, turn notes into conclusions, to-dos, owners, and deadlines. "Delivery" here means first getting a checkable result; only when the same approach is used consistently across similar tasks does it become "reuse"; only when a team shares, authorizes, and maintains it continuously does it enter "governance." This book argues that enterprise AI adoption should extend the focus from one-off model Q&A to verifiable, reusable, governable business workflows.

## When Is a Task Done

<span id="claim-summary-delivery-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-delivery-01"></span>A task counts as done not when text has been generated, but when you hold a result that is editable, verifiable, and transferable. This book argues: generating content is not completing work; only an editable, verifiable, transferable result constitutes an acceptable delivery—see [Chapter 1](/en/qwenwork/ch01-delivery-standard) for the specific criteria. A weekly report must allow checking the numbers, filling the gaps, and handing off to the next colleague; a meeting wrap-up must allow confirming conclusions, owners, and deadlines. For example, an AI-generated weekly report must still be editable, have its numbers checked, and be sendable to the next colleague to keep working on.

## Where Humans Must Confirm

<span id="claim-summary-g3-separation-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-g3-separation-01"></span>Humans must confirm facts and figures, conclusions and reporting conventions, anything involving permissions or sensitive information, and recipients, publishing scope, and the actual act of sending. AI can organize materials and build drafts, but it cannot bear business judgment or publishing responsibility for you. This book recommends: separation of duties between the G3 executor and the action approver. Action grading, authorization, confirmation, and takeover are covered in [Chapter 11](/en/qwenwork/ch11-security-governance); workflow versions, change gates, and operational records in [Chapter 12](/en/qwenwork/ch12-workflow-operations). For example, AI can draft a notice to be sent, but before it actually goes out, an authorized person should check the recipients, content, and impact.

## From One Task to a Reusable Practice

<span id="claim-summary-scene-workflow-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-scene-workflow-01"></span>This book argues: scenarios define the pilot and the value boundary, while workflows are the reusable, operable asset; between the two you must pass through the [Chapter 10 stage gates](/en/qwenwork/ch10-pilot-roadmap)—one success cannot be promoted directly into scaled capability. For example, one smooth data analysis only shows this task produced a usable version; you still need to confirm the data scope, the reviewer, and the conditions for repeated use.

<span id="claim-summary-durable-value-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-durable-value-01"></span>This book argues: long-term value is carried jointly by workflow assets, professional standards, organizational connections, and governance capability, and cannot be replaced by a one-off generation result; operations and measurement methods are covered in [Chapter 12](/en/qwenwork/ch12-workflow-operations) and [Chapter 13](/en/qwenwork/ch13-value-measurement). For example, a monthly report that can be produced consistently over several cycles under a unified basis, with someone checking and maintaining it, says far more about whether it deserves to stay than generating it quickly once.

## From Personal Tasks to Team Use

<span id="claim-summary-capability-map-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-capability-map-01"></span>This book recommends advancing in the order of [complete one delivery](/en/qwenwork/ch01-delivery-standard) → [distill one workflow](/en/qwenwork/ch04-skills-connectors) → [apply it to professional scenarios](/en/qwenwork/ch07-role-roadmaps) → [scale it into an organizational capability](/en/qwenwork/ch12-workflow-operations), with each step's promotion decided by the corresponding standards and evidence. For example, one person first produces a deliverable report, then hands the template to teammates to try, and only then is broader rollout discussed.

## Before Team Use, Revisit Stages, Governance, and Permissions

<span id="claim-summary-window-30-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-window-30-01"></span>This book recommends: days 0–30 validate only personal template candidates; the stage's minimum approvers are the user and the business owner, and the final approver is the union of that set with all applicable hard-gate approvers; use deliverables, acceptance records, and anomaly records as evidence—stop the promotion if any applicable hard gate fails or acceptance is not passed. For example, one person first uses the template for several weekly reports and records what needed fixing; do not let the whole team adopt it just because the first run looked good.

<span id="claim-summary-window-60-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-window-60-01"></span>This book recommends: days 31–60 validate only team workflow releases; the stage's minimum approvers are the business owner, the process maintainer, and the data/security owner, and the final approver is the union of that set with all applicable hard-gate approvers; use workflow versions, team acceptance, approvals, and anomaly records as evidence—stop the release if any applicable hard gate fails; if other release gates are not passed, optimize and retest or stop. For example, when a team pilots a meeting-minutes template, record versions, who performed acceptance, and which anomalies still need manual handling.

<span id="claim-summary-window-90-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-window-90-01"></span>This book recommends: days 61–90 validate only scaled rollout; the stage's minimum approvers are the business owner and the data/security owner, and the final approver is the union of that set with all applicable hard-gate approvers; decide based on run, quality, efficiency, and permission evidence covering a full business cycle—stop the rollout if any applicable hard gate fails, registered targets are not met, or permission issues remain unresolved. For example, before handing a data-analysis practice to multiple departments, first confirm quality, permissions, and anomaly records over a complete business cycle.

<span id="claim-summary-calendar-gate-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-calendar-gate-01"></span>This book argues: 30/60/90 days are merely three verification windows; the calendar cannot replace stage gates, and an unpassed stage must not auto-promote; sample sizes and full thresholds appear only in [Chapter 10](/en/qwenwork/ch10-pilot-roadmap), and this summary does not establish a separate standard. For example, if day 31 arrives but the personal template has not passed acceptance, keep revising or stop—do not release automatically just because the calendar says so.

## Who Owns It, Who Confirms It

<span id="claim-summary-roles-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-roles-01"></span>This book argues: the responsibility chain uses the six role types from [Chapter 11](/en/qwenwork/ch11-security-governance)—business owner, process maintainer, data/security owner, user, content reviewer, and action approver; the business owner is always accountable for the final business outcome. For example, meeting minutes may be organized by the user, but action items involving commitments to customers still need confirmation from the business owner.

## How to Judge Value

<span id="claim-summary-value-reporting-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-value-reporting-01"></span>This book argues: usage volume is not value; per [Chapter 13](/en/qwenwork/ch13-value-measurement), report cash ROI, releasable capacity, quality, and risk separately—do not copy formulas into this summary, and do not write saved hours directly as cash gains. For example, everyone using a reporting template only shows usage volume up; you still need to separately record time saved, rework changes, quality, and risk.

## Evidence Boundaries

<span id="claim-summary-evidence-classes-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-evidence-classes-01"></span>This book recommends: readers first distinguish product facts, customer outcomes, public demos, and community methods, then verify type, source, status, and limitations per [Chapter 8](/en/qwenwork/ch08-research-evidence-chain) and the [claim evidence ledger](/en/qwenwork/appendix-evidence-ledger). For example, a product page can show that a capability exists, but by itself cannot show that your meeting minutes will pass in one shot.

<span id="claim-summary-demo-case-boundary-01" data-claim-id="claim-summary-demo-case-boundary-01"></span>This book argues: public demos cannot be extrapolated into customer outcomes; case counts are decided only by [Chapter 9](/en/qwenwork/ch09-public-case-atlas) and the [case source map](/en/qwenwork/appendix-case-source-map), and this summary adds no strong factual commitments about customer names, customer outcomes, precise effect numbers, or case counts. For example, seeing a fast web demo only helps you understand the shape of the deliverable; you cannot write a customer statement directly as a verified outcome.
